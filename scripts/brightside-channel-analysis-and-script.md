## 1. What the channel actually is

Bright Side is an animation-and-stock-footage "curiosity" channel. One male narrator (Donald Moffit-style: warm, upbeat, slightly goofy) talks directly to *you* over simple 2D animation, stock video, and on-screen text. No faces, no host, no interviews. Every video is a single, instantly understandable promise that a 12-year-old and a 60-year-old would both click.

Core content pillars (in order of how often they post them):

1. **"What if…" scenario videos** — a hypothetical played out step by step (what if you fell into a black hole / stopped sleeping / the Earth stopped spinning).
2. **"Scientists found…" discovery videos** — recent 2026 examples: *"You Won't Believe What Scientists Found in the Deepest Place on Earth"*, *"Scientists Just Made These 5 Incredible Discoveries in 2026"*, *"Unexplained Real Events That Shocked Scientists"*, *"Scientists Found Something Massive Beneath Texas."*
3. **Survival / body tips** — "X Tips to Survive…", "What Your Body Does When…".
4. **Space + planet trivia**, **historical mysteries**, and **riddles / puzzle tests** (their spin-off channels now carry most of the riddles).

## 2. The title formula

Almost every title is one of five templates. Copy the template, not the words.

| Template | Example shape | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario | "What If You Fell Into ___" | Viewer instantly imagines themselves in it |
| Authority reveal | "Scientists Found ___ and Nobody Expected It" | Borrowed credibility + curiosity gap |
| Countdown list | "13 Tips to Survive ___" | Odd number, scannable, promise of completeness |
| Body / everyday | "What Happens to Your Body If You ___ Every Day" | Personal relevance |
| Warning | "Never Do ___ If You See ___" | Fear + usefulness |

Rules they follow: 6 to 12 words, present tense, "You/Your" whenever possible, one concrete noun the thumbnail can draw (a trench, a whale, a hole), and no numbers in the title unless it's a list.

## 3. The hook (first 20 seconds)

Bright Side does **not** introduce the topic. The narrator drops you straight into the scenario in second person, present tense, then ends the hook with a question the whole video answers.

Pattern:

1. **Place the viewer in the scene** ("You're standing on the deck of a boat 200 miles from Guam…").
2. **One jaw-drop comparison** ("Mount Everest would fit in here with over a mile to spare").
3. **The stakes question** ("So what actually happens to your body on the way down?").

No channel intro, no "today we're going to". The logo sting comes *after* the hook, if at all.

## 4. Body structure (the "scenario ladder")

Long videos are chopped into 8 to 12 short beats, each 40 to 70 seconds. Every beat does three things:

- **Advances the scenario** one step (you sink another 1,000 meters).
- **Pays off one fact** with a visual comparison (Styrofoam cup shrinks to a thimble).
- **Ends on a mini-cliffhanger** ("…but the real danger hasn't even started yet").

Between beats they use verbal signposts: "Okay, but…", "Now here's the weird part.", "Hold on.", "Let's go deeper." Language sits at an 8th-grade level. Sentences are short. The narrator jokes at himself about once a minute (keeps it friendly, not lecture-y).

## 5. Visual grammar

- Flat 2D animation of a generic character (often the viewer-avatar) + stock footage + big on-screen numbers.
- Every number gets a visual analogy (elephants, football fields, Eiffel Towers).
- Text callouts for the key fact of each beat so it works with sound off.
- Thumbnail: one huge object + small human for scale + a shocked cartoon face or arrow, 3 to 5 words max.

## 6. Ending

Never a summary. Two moves:

1. A quick "what would *you* do" question for the comments.
2. A pointer to a specific related video ("if you want to know what's at the bottom of the deepest hole on land, that video is right here").

Then a soft like/share ask. Total outro: under 25 seconds.

## 7. Topic picked for this script

The channel's newest upload is about the deepest place on Earth, so the trend is live. This script takes the same setting but flips it into their strongest template: a second-person **What If** journey. It is a new angle, not a copy of that video.

Alternate on-brand topics if you want a different one (all follow the same script skeleton):

# SCRIPT

SCRIPT: What If You Fell Into the Mariana Trench?
Channel style: Bright Side (animated explainer, single narrator)
Target length: 9 to 10 minutes
Estimated word count: ~1,450 (narrator at ~150 wpm)

────────────────────────────────────────

[ACT 1 — COLD OPEN — 0:00-0:20]

[VISUAL: Calm blue ocean, tiny boat from above. Camera tilts down and the water goes from turquoise to black. A small cartoon "you" splashes in.]

[VOICEOVER]: You're floating in warm water about 200 miles from the island of Guam. The sun is out. It looks like any other beach day.

Except there's no beach. Under your feet there's nothing but water for almost 7 miles.

[VISUAL: Mount Everest slides into the trench and disappears with room to spare.]

If you dropped Mount Everest in here, its peak would still sit more than a mile below the surface.

[PAUSE]

So here's the question. If you started sinking right now, what would happen to you on the way down? And what's waiting at the bottom?

Let's find out.

[SFX: bubbles, muffled splash]

────────────────────────────────────────

[ACT 2 — SETUP — 0:20-1:20]

[VISUAL: Map of the Pacific, Mariana Trench highlighted as a crescent. Two tectonic plates, one sliding under the other.]

[VOICEOVER]: This is the Mariana Trench. It's a crescent-shaped scar in the floor of the Pacific Ocean, about 1,500 miles long.

It exists because two giant slabs of Earth's crust are crashing into each other here. The Pacific plate loses the fight and slides underneath, dragging the seafloor down with it.

The deepest point has a name: Challenger Deep. Depth: roughly 36,000 feet. That's around 10,900 meters.

[VISUAL: Cartoon "you" in swim trunks, looking down nervously. A tiny scale bar appears.]

Now, for this to work, let's pretend you can breathe underwater, and you sink at a slow, steady pace. Kind of like a very unlucky rock.

[VISUAL: On-screen text: "Sunlight Zone – 0 to 200 m"]

At the surface, the water is a cozy 82 degrees Fahrenheit. Fish, coral, sea turtles. Everything you'd expect.

Enjoy it. This is the last part of the trip that looks like a nature documentary.

────────────────────────────────────────

[ACT 3 — THE BUILD — 1:20-7:30]

**Beat 1 — 10 meters: the first squeeze (1:20-1:55)**

[VISUAL: Pressure gauge next to "you". A balloon shrinks to half size.]

[VOICEOVER]: You've only dropped about 33 feet, the depth of a tall diving pool, and the pressure on your body has already doubled.

Every 33 feet you sink, the ocean adds the weight of another entire atmosphere on top of you.

Your lungs are basically two balloons. At this depth they're already half the size they were at the surface.

[VISUAL: The balloon keeps shrinking as the depth counter climbs.]

Okay, but you're still fine. Free divers go way past this. Keep going.

**Beat 2 — 200 meters: the lights go out (1:55-2:35)**

[VISUAL: Water darkens to deep navy. On-screen text: "Twilight Zone – 200 to 1,000 m"]

[VOICEOVER]: At around 650 feet, the sunlight gives up. From here on, it's twilight. Then it's just dark.

This is also roughly where the human body's limit is. The deepest a person has ever gone on a single breath is about 700 feet, and the deepest scuba dive ever recorded is just over 1,000 feet.

[VISUAL: Cartoon world-record diver waves goodbye and floats back up. "You" keeps sinking.]

Both of those people had to come back up very, very slowly to survive. You? You're not coming back up. Sorry.

[VISUAL: Styrofoam cup demo: a normal cup next to a shrunken, wrinkled one the size of a thimble.]

Here's a fun experiment scientists actually do. They tie a Styrofoam cup to a deep-sea camera. When it comes back, the cup is the size of a thimble. Same cup. The pressure just squeezed all the air out of the foam.

Remember that cup. It's about to matter.

**Beat 3 — 1,000 meters: you have company (2:35-3:20)**

[VISUAL: Pitch black. Small glowing dots blink on. Anglerfish, lanternfish, glowing jellyfish.]

[VOICEOVER]: Welcome to the midnight zone. It's now completely black. The only light comes from the animals themselves.

About three out of every four creatures down here make their own glow. Some use it as a flashlight. Some use it as bait. Some flash it to confuse whatever's trying to eat them.

[VISUAL: Sperm whale glides past "you", a squid in its mouth.]

And then there's this guy. Sperm whales dive down here to hunt giant squid. They can hold their breath for over an hour and reach more than 6,000 feet.

[VISUAL: On-screen: "Water temperature: 39°F / 4°C"]

The water temperature has dropped to just above freezing. If you were a real human without our magic rules, you'd have stopped feeling your hands and feet a while ago.

[PAUSE]

Now here's the weird part. Your body isn't crushed like a soda can. Not yet, anyway.

**Beat 4 — Why you don't pop (3:20-4:00)**

[VISUAL: Cross-section of a cartoon body. Water-filled parts glow blue; air pockets (lungs, sinuses, ears) glow red.]

[VOICEOVER]: Your body is mostly water, and water barely compresses at all. So your muscles, your blood, your bones? They're fine.

The problem is the air pockets. Lungs. Sinuses. Your middle ear. Those are the Styrofoam cup.

By now they've been squeezed down to a tiny fraction of their normal size. In real life, that's what actually ends the trip for a diver, long before any "crushing" happens.

[VISUAL: Cartoon "you" shrugs, lungs shown as two raisins.]

But we said you can breathe water. So you're a very cold, very confused, mostly fine human. Let's keep going.

**Beat 5 — 4,000 meters: the snow that never melts (4:00-4:45)**

[VISUAL: Slow drifting white flakes in the dark. On-screen text: "Abyssal Zone – 4,000 to 6,000 m"]

[VOICEOVER]: It's snowing. Seriously.

Scientists call this marine snow. It's tiny bits of dead plants, dead animals, and, well, fish poop, slowly drifting down from the surface. It can take weeks for one flake to reach the bottom.

Down here, this "snow" is the main food supply. Entire ecosystems live on leftovers from miles above.

[VISUAL: Flat, muddy plain stretching into darkness. A sea cucumber crawls by.]

The ground here is a huge, flat plain of soft mud. Most of the ocean floor on Earth looks exactly like this. Quiet, cold, and dark, basically forever.

You'd think this is the bottom. It isn't. The trench is about to drop away underneath you.

**Beat 6 — 6,000 meters: the hadal zone (4:45-5:35)**

[VISUAL: The seafloor splits and "you" tumbles into a steep V-shaped canyon. On-screen text: "Hadal Zone – 6,000 to 11,000 m". Name callout: "Hadal, from Hades, the Greek underworld."]

[VOICEOVER]: This is the hadal zone, named after Hades, the Greek god of the underworld. Yeah, they weren't being subtle.

Almost nothing from the outside world has ever been here. Fewer people have reached the bottom of this trench than have walked on the Moon.

[VISUAL: Pale pink snailfish with tiny fins hovers in a headlight beam.]

But something lives here. In 2023, a camera filmed a small, ghost-colored snailfish swimming at about 27,000 feet. That's the deepest fish anyone has ever seen.

It has no scales. It has no swim bladder. Its body is soft and jelly-like, so there's nothing for the pressure to crush. It's basically a fish that solved the Styrofoam cup problem.

[PAUSE]

And it's not alone.

**Beat 7 — The bottom: what's actually down there (5:35-6:40)**

[VISUAL: "You" lands softly in silt. A cloud of mud rises. A submersible headlight sweeps across.]

[VOICEOVER]: You've arrived. Challenger Deep. About 36,000 feet down. The trip took you roughly four hours.

[VISUAL: Big on-screen number: "8 TONS per square inch". A cartoon elephant balances on a postage stamp.]

The pressure here is over a thousand times what it is at the surface. Around 8 tons pressing on every square inch of you. Picture a full-grown elephant standing on a postage stamp. Now cover your entire body in those stamps.

[VISUAL: Tiny shrimp-like amphipods swarm in the light. A giant white blob, the xenophyophore, sits on the mud.]

So what's down here? Little shrimp-like creatures called amphipods, scavenging anything that drifts down. Sea cucumbers. And these: single-celled organisms the size of your fist. One cell. Big as an apple. Scientists still argue about how they work.

[VISUAL: Cartoon plastic bag drifts past "you" in the beam. Freeze frame.]

And one more thing. When explorers reached the bottom, they found a plastic bag. And candy wrappers.

Humans have visited this place only a handful of times. Our trash beat us here.

**Beat 8 — The people who actually did this (6:40-7:30)**

[VISUAL: 1960 archival-style illustration: the bathyscaphe Trieste, two men in a tiny sphere. Then 2012: a lime-green vertical sub. Then 2019: a white two-seater sub.]

[VOICEOVER]: Only a few humans have seen this with their own eyes.

In 1960, two men squeezed into a steel ball hanging under a giant float called the Trieste. It took nearly five hours to get down. A window cracked on the way. They stayed twenty minutes and came back up.

In 2012, filmmaker James Cameron went down alone in a sub shaped like a lime-green torpedo.

Then in 2019, an explorer named Victor Vescovo went down several times in a row. He's the guy who reported the plastic.

[VISUAL: Sub rising, "you" waving from the mud.]

Every one of those vehicles had walls thick enough to stop a tank shell. You had swim trunks.

────────────────────────────────────────

[ACT 4 — CLIMAX — 7:30-8:45]

[VISUAL: Slow pull back. "You" gets smaller and smaller. The camera rises through every zone we passed, fast, back to blue sky.]

[VOICEOVER]: So let's drop the magic rules for a second and be honest about what would really happen.

[VISUAL: Depth ticker runs alongside each line.]

At 30 feet, your ears would hurt.

At a few hundred feet, the pressure would push the air out of your lungs and you'd black out.

By the midnight zone, the cold would have finished the job.

And by the time your body reached the bottom, hours later, it would be almost unchanged. No crushing. No pancake. Just very cold, very still, and about to be dinner for a swarm of amphipods.

[PAUSE]

[VISUAL: Wide shot: the trench cross-section with the tiny dot of "you" at the bottom, Everest ghosted in for scale.]

That's the strange thing about the deepest place on Earth. It doesn't destroy you with drama. It just keeps you. Forever. In the dark.

And we've explored less of it than we've explored the surface of Mars.

────────────────────────────────────────

[ACT 5 — OUTCOME + NEXT VIDEO — 8:45-9:30]

[VISUAL: "You" pops back up at the surface, gasping, sunburned, holding the tiny shrunken cup.]

[VOICEOVER]: Good news: you made it back. Bad news: you now own the world's smallest coffee cup.

[VISUAL: Split screen: Mariana Trench on the left, a rusty drill rig on frozen ground on the right.]

Here's what we want to know. Would you rather take this trip down into the ocean, or get lowered into the deepest hole humans ever drilled on land? Because that one goes so deep the drill bits started melting. Tell us in the comments.

And if you want to see what scientists found at the bottom of *that* hole, the video is right here.

[VISUAL: End card with two video thumbnails.]

If you learned something new today, hit like and send this to the one friend who's afraid of deep water.

See you next time.

────────────────────────────────────────
