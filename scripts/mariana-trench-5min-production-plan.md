## Blocks

Each block: 15 seconds of video, ~37 words of VO. VO text is exactly what goes to the TTS model. Video prompt is the Seedance 2.0 prompt (append the style lock).

### Block 01 — Cold open (0:00–0:15)
**VO:** You're floating in warm water, two hundred miles from the island of Guam. Sun's out. Looks like a normal beach day. Except there's no beach. Under your feet, there's nothing but water for almost seven miles.
**Video:** Top-down view of a calm turquoise ocean, tiny cartoon swimmer in orange trunks floating on the surface, camera slowly tilts and pushes down past the swimmer as the water fades from turquoise to deep navy to black, small sun glints on the surface shrinking above.

### Block 02 — The Everest comparison (0:15–0:30)
**VO:** If you dropped Mount Everest in here, its peak would still sit more than a mile below the surface. So here's the question. If you started sinking right now, what happens on the way down? And what's waiting at the bottom?
**Video:** Side-view cross-section of the ocean as a deep V-shaped trench, a stylized snowy Mount Everest lowers into the trench and its summit stops well below the wavy surface line, a tiny swimmer figure floats at the top for scale, camera slowly zooms out to show the full depth.

### Block 03 — Why the trench exists (0:30–0:45)
**VO:** This is the Mariana Trench. A crescent-shaped scar in the Pacific floor, fifteen hundred miles long. Two giant slabs of Earth's crust crash together here. The Pacific plate loses, slides underneath, and drags the seafloor down with it.
**Video:** Stylized map of the western Pacific with a glowing crescent marking the trench, then cut to a cross-section where two thick rock plates push together and one slowly slides beneath the other, seafloor bending downward, small bubbles rising, camera drifts right.

### Block 04 — The rules of the trip (0:45–1:00)
**VO:** The deepest point is called Challenger Deep. Thirty-six thousand feet down. For this trip, let's pretend you can breathe water, and you sink slowly, like a very unlucky rock. Up here the water's a cozy eighty-two degrees.
**Video:** Cartoon swimmer in orange trunks hanging upright in bright sunlit shallow water, colorful fish and a sea turtle glide past, sunbeams ripple, a small thermometer graphic floats beside them, swimmer gives a nervous thumbs-up, camera slowly descends.

### Block 05 — 10 meters: the squeeze (1:00–1:15)
**VO:** Thirty-three feet down, the depth of a deep pool, and the pressure on your body has already doubled. Every thirty-three feet adds the weight of another whole atmosphere on top of you. Your lungs? Two balloons, already half their size.
**Video:** Cartoon swimmer sinking in mid-blue water, a large translucent balloon beside them slowly shrinks to half its size, faint pressure rings pulse inward around the swimmer, bubbles drift up, camera holds steady then tilts down.

### Block 06 — 200 meters: lights out (1:15–1:30)
**VO:** At six hundred fifty feet, the sunlight gives up. Twilight, then black. This is also roughly the human limit. The deepest breath-hold dive ever is about seven hundred feet. The deepest scuba dive, just over a thousand.
**Video:** The water darkens from blue to deep navy as the swimmer sinks, a second cartoon diver in a wetsuit waves goodbye and floats back up toward the fading light, our swimmer keeps sinking into darkness, last rays of light shrink to a distant glow above.

### Block 07 — The Styrofoam cup (1:30–1:45)
**VO:** Here's an experiment scientists really do. They tie a foam cup to a deep-sea camera. When it comes back up, the cup is the size of a thimble. Same cup. The pressure squeezed all the air out. Remember that cup.
**Video:** Close-up of a white foam cup tied to a boxy deep-sea camera rig, the rig descends into darkness, then the cup visibly shrinks and wrinkles down to thimble size beside a normal cup for comparison, spotlight beam, dark blue background.

### Block 08 — 1,000 meters: the midnight zone (1:45–2:00)
**VO:** Welcome to the midnight zone. Total darkness. The only light comes from the animals themselves. Three out of four creatures down here make their own glow. Some use it as a flashlight. Some use it as bait.
**Video:** Pitch-black water, dozens of tiny bioluminescent dots blink on, a cartoon anglerfish with a glowing lure drifts past, lanternfish and a glowing jellyfish pulse softly, the swimmer's silhouette is lit only by the creatures' glow, slow drift.

### Block 09 — The sperm whale (2:00–2:15)
**VO:** And then there's this guy. Sperm whales dive down here to hunt giant squid. They hold their breath for over an hour and reach more than six thousand feet. The water is now just above freezing. Your body, though? Not crushed. Yet.
**Video:** A huge stylized sperm whale glides slowly past the tiny cartoon swimmer in the dark, a squid tentacle trailing from its mouth, faint blue rim light, ice-blue particles drift, the whale's eye passes close to camera, camera follows the whale's length.

### Block 10 — Why you don't pop (2:15–2:30)
**VO:** Your body is mostly water, and water barely compresses. Muscles, blood, bones? Fine. The problem is the air pockets. Lungs. Sinuses. Ears. Those are the foam cup. By now they're squeezed to a fraction of their size.
**Video:** Simple cross-section diagram of the cartoon swimmer, body glowing calm blue, the lungs, sinuses and ears glowing orange, the orange areas slowly shrink while the blue stays the same, gentle pulsing pressure rings, dark background, slow zoom in.

### Block 11 — 4,000 meters: marine snow (2:30–2:45)
**VO:** It's snowing. Seriously. Scientists call it marine snow: tiny bits of dead plants, animals, and, well, fish poop, drifting down from the surface. One flake can take weeks to land. Down here, this snow is the whole food supply.
**Video:** Slow-motion white flakes drift down through black water like snowfall, the cartoon swimmer looks up with a flake landing on their nose, a sea cucumber crawls across a flat muddy plain below, camera slowly descends with the flakes.

### Block 12 — The floor drops away (2:45–3:00)
**VO:** The ground here is a huge flat plain of soft mud. Most of the ocean floor on Earth looks exactly like this. Quiet, cold, and dark forever. You'd think this is the bottom. It isn't. The floor is about to drop away.
**Video:** Wide shot of a flat grey-blue mud plain in dim light, the cartoon swimmer drifts across it, then the seafloor ahead splits into a steep V-shaped canyon falling into total blackness, the swimmer tumbles over the edge, camera tilts down into the void.

### Block 13 — The hadal zone (3:00–3:15)
**VO:** This is the hadal zone. Named after Hades, god of the underworld. They weren't being subtle. Fewer people have reached the bottom of this trench than have walked on the Moon. But something lives here.
**Video:** The swimmer sinks between towering dark canyon walls, faint teal light on the rock edges, a stylized crescent moon and tiny astronaut fade in as a ghosted comparison then fade out, a pale glow appears far below, camera descends slowly.

### Block 14 — The deepest fish (3:15–3:30)
**VO:** In 2023, a camera filmed a small ghost-colored snailfish swimming at twenty-seven thousand feet. The deepest fish anyone has ever seen. No scales. No swim bladder. Soft, jelly-like body. Nothing for the pressure to crush. And it's not alone.
**Video:** A single pale pink translucent snailfish with a tadpole-shaped body and tiny fins hovers in a headlight beam against black water, it slowly turns toward camera, delicate jelly-like skin, a second snailfish drifts in from the edge, slow push in.

### Block 15 — Touchdown (3:30–3:45)
**VO:** You've arrived. Challenger Deep. Thirty-six thousand feet down. The trip took about four hours. The pressure is over a thousand times what it is at the surface. Around eight tons on every square inch of you.
**Video:** The cartoon swimmer lands softly in pale silt, a cloud of fine sediment puffs up around them, then cut to a cartoon elephant balancing on a single tiny postage stamp, the stamp multiplies to cover the swimmer's silhouette, dark background, spotlight.

### Block 16 — What's down here (3:45–4:00)
**VO:** So what's down here? Little shrimp-like scavengers called amphipods. Sea cucumbers. And single-celled blobs the size of your fist. One cell, big as an apple. Scientists still argue about how they work. And one more thing.
**Video:** Headlight beam sweeps across the trench floor, a swarm of tiny pale shrimp-like amphipods scatters, a purple sea cucumber inches along, a lumpy white fist-sized blob sits on the mud, the beam stops on something at the edge of frame, slow pan.

### Block 17 — The plastic bag (4:00–4:15)
**VO:** When explorers reached the bottom, they found a plastic bag. And candy wrappers. Humans have visited this place only a handful of times. Our trash beat us here. And every sub that made it down had walls thick enough to stop a tank shell.
**Video:** A crumpled translucent plastic bag drifts slowly through the headlight beam in the black water beside the cartoon swimmer, who stares at it, then a stylized round white two-seat submersible with a thick porthole descends into frame, lights on, camera holds.

### Block 18 — What would really happen (4:15–4:30)
**VO:** Now drop the magic rules. At thirty feet your ears hurt. A few hundred feet, the pressure pushes the air out of your lungs and you black out. The cold finishes the job. Hours later you reach the bottom, almost unchanged.
**Video:** Fast vertical descent through every zone in one continuous shot, bright blue to twilight to black to canyon walls, a small depth marker line rises up the side of the frame, the cartoon swimmer goes from waving to limp to a still silhouette landing on silt.

### Block 19 — The quiet ending (4:30–4:45)
**VO:** No crushing. No pancake. Just very cold, very still, and about to be dinner for a swarm of amphipods. The deepest place on Earth doesn't destroy you with drama. It just keeps you. Forever. In the dark.
**Video:** Extreme wide cross-section of the whole trench, a single tiny dot of the swimmer at the very bottom, a ghosted Mount Everest outline for scale, the last light fades from the frame edges until only the dot and faint canyon lines remain, very slow zoom out.

### Block 20 — Outro (4:45–5:00)
**VO:** Good news: you made it back. Bad news: you now own the world's smallest coffee cup. Would you rather do this, or get lowered into the deepest hole ever drilled on land? Tell us below. That video is right here.
**Video:** The cartoon swimmer pops up at the bright sunny surface, gasping, sunburned, proudly holding a tiny shrunken foam cup, then the frame splits: ocean trench on the left, a rusty drill rig on frozen tundra on the right, both with an empty space for an end-card thumbnail.
